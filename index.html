Lagos retail sales analysis

A fictional six-branch supermarket chain with a deliberately messy 2025 sales file, containing duplicates, missing unit counts, negative revenue and inconsistent branch names. I cleaned it, then asked what drives revenue.

What I found

Cleaning removed 25 duplicate rows, rebuilt 20 missing unit counts from revenue and price, corrected 10 negative revenue entries and standardised the branch names, taking 13,165 rows down to 13,140. Total revenue is ₦2.36 billion. December is the peak at ₦267M. Weekends earn 23.7% more per day than weekdays. Victoria Island leads with 20.0% of sales and Ajah trails with 13.1%, about ₦164M a year behind. Electronics is the top category at 26.2% of revenue from the fewest units.

Where it falls short

The file is simulated. Discounts showed no lift in units sold, but they were assigned at random, so a flat result is expected here. On real data that is the first thing I would test properly.

Nigeria in the Gapminder data

How did Nigeria's life expectancy, income and population change from 1952 to 2007, and how does that compare with Africa and the world? Then a harder question: does income explain the gap? This is real data, so the answers are about what happened and not about what I would have liked to find.

What I found

Nigeria's life expectancy rose 10.5 years between 1952 and 1987, then did not move: 46.9 in 1987 and 46.9 in 2007. The African median rose another 1.3 years over the same twenty years, and by 2007 stood at 52.9, with the world median at 71.9. Population grew 4.1 times (33 million to 135 million, about 2.6% a year, the same pace as Africa overall) and income per person grew 1.9 times, close to the African median of 1.75 times.

Across 142 countries in 2007, every doubling of income goes with about 5.0 more years of life expectancy (95% range 4.5 to 5.5, R² 0.65). At Nigeria's income of $2,014 that line predicts 59.7 years. Nigeria's actual figure is 46.9, which is 12.9 years below the line and the 10th largest shortfall of the 142 countries. Countries at similar income, such as Vietnam, Nicaragua and India, live far longer.

Where it falls short

The data stops in 2007, so this describes a long period and not Nigeria today. It shows what happened, not why. It cannot say what held life expectancy back after 1987, and a trend fitted across countries is not a promise about what would happen to one country if its income rose. Income is a national average and says nothing about how it is shared. Values are Gapminder estimates at five-year steps.

Sales report automation

Cleaning a sales file and building the same summary tables and chart by hand takes an hour every time. This script does it with one command and leaves a log of every change, so the person reading the report can trust it.

What it does

The script reads the CSV and stops with a clear message if a column is missing. It removes duplicate rows, standardises branch and category names, rebuilds missing unit counts from revenue and price, and corrects negative revenue. Every fix is counted in the Cleaning log. It then writes one formatted Excel workbook: a summary, revenue by month with a native Excel chart, by branch and by category, the log, and the clean data with filters.

How I checked it

Total revenue in the report is ₦2,362,697,570.99 and units sold are 685,763. Both match, to the kobo, the separate analysis in the retail project, which was written independently. The monthly figures match too.

Where it falls short

It expects one file layout, so a different layout needs the column list at the top of the script changed. The report holds values, not live Excel formulas. The sample file is simulated.

def clean(raw):
    """Return the clean frame and a list of (issue, rows affected, what was done)."""
    log = [("Rows in the raw file", len(raw), "")]
    df = raw.copy()

    n = int(df["date"].isna().sum())
    df = df.dropna(subset=["date"])
    log.append(("Rows with an unreadable date", n, "Removed" if n else "None found"))

    n = int(df.duplicated().sum())
    df = df.drop_duplicates()
    log.append(("Exact duplicate rows", n, "Removed" if n else "None found"))

    for col in ("branch", "category"):
        tidy = df[col].astype(str).str.strip().str.title()
        n = int((tidy != df[col]).sum())
        df[col] = tidy
        log.append((f"Inconsistent {col} names (spacing or capitals)", n, "Standardised" if n else "None found"))

    n = int(df["units_sold"].isna().sum())
    rebuild = df["units_sold"].isna() & df["unit_price_ngn"].notna()
    df.loc[rebuild, "units_sold"] = (
        df.loc[rebuild, "revenue_ngn"].abs()
        / (df.loc[rebuild, "unit_price_ngn"] * (1 - df.loc[rebuild, "discount"]))
    ).round()
    still = int(df["units_sold"].isna().sum())
    df = df.dropna(subset=["units_sold"])
    log.append(("Missing unit counts", n,
                f"Rebuilt from revenue and price ({n - still}), removed ({still})" if n else "None found"))

    n = int((df["revenue_ngn"] < 0).sum())
    df["revenue_ngn"] = df["revenue_ngn"].abs()
    log.append(("Negative revenue values", n, "Sign corrected" if n else "None found"))

    df["units_sold"] = df["units_sold"].astype(int)
    log.append(("Rows in the clean data", len(df), ""))
    return df.reset_index(drop=True), log

Telecom churn prediction

Predicting who will leave is the easy half. The harder half is deciding who to call. I built the model on 7,000 simulated customers, then chose the alert threshold by the naira cost of wrong calls instead of by accuracy.

What I found

Logistic regression (AUC 0.75) beat gradient boosting (0.73). Raising the threshold from 0.5 to 0.575 cut the bill from ₦15.60M to ₦15.25M, which is 7.9% below doing nothing. At that setting the model caught 233 of 368 churners, missed 135 and sent 248 offers to people who would have stayed. Contract type is the strongest signal, with two-year customers far less likely to leave.

Where it falls short

The saving is modest and it rests on my assumptions. If an offer keeps fewer than about 28% of the churners it reaches, calling customers costs more than it saves. The data is simulated, so real customer data would need its own checks.

def total_cost(y_true, prob, thr):
    flag = prob >= thr
    churn = y_true.values == 1
    cost = np.where(flag & churn, OFFER + (1 - SAVE_RATE) * LOSS, 0.0)
    cost += np.where(flag & ~churn, OFFER, 0.0)
    cost += np.where(~flag & churn, LOSS, 0.0)
    return float(cost.sum())

Churn risk checker

A model that stays in a notebook helps nobody. This is the churn model from the project above, packaged as a page a retention team could open: enter a customer's details, then see the risk score, whether it crosses the offer line, and which details push it up or down. Try it.

What it does

I trained a logistic regression in Python, then exported it as a small file of numbers: one weight for each of its twelve inputs, the scaling values and the intercept. The page applies those numbers in JavaScript, so there is no server and no customer data is sent anywhere.

How I checked it

The project includes 25 test customers scored by scikit-learn. A test script runs the JavaScript scorer on the same customers and compares the results. The largest difference is under 0.000001, so the page gives the same answers as the Python model.

Where it falls short

The model is class-weighted, so its score ranks customers and is not a literal chance of leaving. The offer line comes from the cost analysis in the churn project, which assumes ₦45,000 lost per churner and ₦6,000 per offer. The data is simulated. A real deployment would need real data, monitoring and a way to update the model.

function scoreCustomer(model, c) {
  var z = model.intercept;
  var parts = {};

  model.features.forEach(function (feature, i) {
    var kind = feature.slice(0, 1);
    var name = feature.slice(3);
    var x;
    var group;

    if (kind === "n") {
      x = (c[name] - model.numeric_mean[name]) / model.numeric_scale[name];
      group = name === "monthly_charge_ngn" ? "plan" : name;
    } else {
      var cut = name.indexOf("_");
      var column = name.slice(0, cut);
      x = c[column] === name.slice(cut + 1) ? 1 : 0;
      group = column;
    }

    z += model.coef[i] * x;
    parts[group] = (parts[group] || 0) + model.coef[i] * (x - model.feature_mean[i]);
  });

  var score = 1 / (1 + Math.exp(-z));
  var factors = Object.keys(parts).map(function (k) { return { key: k, value: parts[k] }; });
  return { score: score, flag: score >= model.threshold, factors: factors };
}

Breast cancer classifier

Can measurements taken from a cell sample tell a malignant tumour from a benign one? I compared three models on 569 real cases and kept 20% of the data aside until the very end.

What I found

A plain logistic regression on scaled features did as well as the two tree ensembles. I picked it using cross-validation on the training data, then scored it once on the test set. It flagged 39 of 42 malignant cases and raised one false alarm among 72 benign ones. The random forest leaned most on the worst perimeter, worst area and worst concave points of the cell nuclei.

Where it falls short

Three malignant cases were missed at the default cutoff. In a screening setting that is the number to push down, by lowering the cutoff and accepting more false alarms. The test set is small, so a single case moves recall by more than two points. This is a learning project on a benchmark dataset and not a diagnostic tool.

Diabetes progression model

Given ten baseline measurements for 442 patients (age, sex, BMI, blood pressure and six blood serum values), predict how far their disease has progressed a year later. I compared linear models and tree ensembles against a baseline that always guesses the average.

What I found

Every real model beat the guess-the-average baseline, cutting typical error by about 27%, and they all landed close together. Ridge, chosen by cross-validation, explains about 48% of the variation. The heavier models did not help, which tells me the limit is the information in ten measurements and not the choice of algorithm. BMI and blood pressure are the clearest drivers.

Where it falls short

About half of the variation is left unexplained, so this could rank patients by risk but could not predict an individual score reliably. Two blood serum measurements (s1 and s2) move together, so their large opposite coefficients should not be read one at a time.

Wine clustering

No labels, only chemistry. I scaled 13 chemical measurements for 178 Italian wines, asked KMeans to find groups, and only afterwards compared the groups with the three real grape cultivars.

What I found

Three clusters scored best, and they match the real cultivars closely, with an adjusted Rand index of 0.90 where 1.0 is a perfect match. Flavanoids, the OD280/OD315 ratio, proline, total phenols and colour intensity separate the groups most.

Where it falls short

A silhouette of 0.29 means the groups touch rather than sit far apart, and a two-dimensional picture shows only 55% of the variation. The match to the cultivars is strong, not perfect.
